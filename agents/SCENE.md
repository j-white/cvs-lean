# The Hall of Primes

A scene description for an immersive WebGL experience that explains the Riemann Hypothesis, and the
path law inside it, to people with no mathematical background.

**The picture:**

> Picture a hallway lined with ruler markings. Every prime number moves along it in its own stride:
> 2 lands on 2, 4, 8, 16…; 3 lands on 3, 9, 27…. Each footstep pulls the floor down a little, while
> a smooth force lifts it. Under the floor sit springs, one for each zeta zero. If a spring bounced
> with growing amplitude, it would crack the floor. The Riemann Hypothesis says every spring stays
> steady, so the floor never cracks.

**The page:** [docs/index.html](../docs/index.html) tells this in seven steps, one sentence each.
It is a single file (three.js 0.160 from a CDN, no other dependency) and it is only the story:
there is no second mode, no settings panel and no URL parameter. The last step's button reads
*Start over* and returns to step 1.

**Files:** `docs/` is the published site (run it with `python3 -m http.server -d docs 8000`).
`docs/hero.png` is the README's screenshot of the page and must be a true screenshot.
`docs/share.png` (the link-preview image, 1200×627) and `docs/poster.png` (1200×1200) are the
owner's poster; do not regenerate, overwrite or re-encode them. `docs/figures/section6.svg` is the
README's §6 figure, generated by `docs/figures/section6.py`; change the script, not the SVG.

## Audience and goals

- **Who:** curious non-mathematicians, on a phone or a laptop, arriving from a social post.
- **What they should leave with:** each prime is simple on its own; the hard part, and the beauty,
  is how they fit together; and that this was proved via Lean 4 in November 2026.
- **How it should feel:** calm, tactile, musical. Something beautiful happens in the first five
  seconds, before any reading.
- **Two misreadings the story heads off:**
  - *The footprints land on multiples (2, 4, 6, 8).* They land on powers (2, 4, 8, 16), because
    the floor is a slide rule: an equal step multiplies. Steps 1 and 2 show this before anything
    else.
  - *The springs sit at the footprints, or at the zeros' positions in the hallway.* A zero is not
    a place in the hallway. It is a rate: how fast its spring bounces as the far wall moves out.
    Step 4 labels the springs with their zeros' heights and moves the far wall so the rates show.

## The story

Each step adds one idea. *Back* and *Next*, or the arrow keys, move between them. The **Says**
lines quote the card copy in the page's `STORY` array, which is the owner's wording: do not
change or "restore" it, and keep this section in step with the array.

The viewer never sees the letter c. Where a length is shown, the wording is *Far wall at N* with N
a whole number. Below, c is the number the far wall stands at and L = log c.

### 1. A slide-rule floor

- **Says:** *Picture a hallway lined with ruler markings.* On this ruler, doubling always takes
  the same length of floor: 1 to 2, 2 to 4 and 4 to 8 are equally far apart.
- **Shows:** the floor from above, ticked at 1, 2, 3, …, 10, 20, …, and the walker 2 alone, hopping
  in equal steps with its landings numbered 2, 4, 8, 16, …. Far wall at 200.

### 2. Every prime walks

- **Says:** *Every prime number moves along it in its own stride: 2 lands on 2, 4, 8, 16…; 3 lands
  on 3, 9, 27….* Every footprint is a power of a prime. The bigger the prime, the longer its
  stride, so big primes cross in a single step.
- **Shows:** 2 walks, then 3 joins with its landings numbered, then every prime below 200 fades
  in, lane by lane. Each small prime sets off a beat after the one before it, so neighbouring
  walkers never move in step. The large primes cross as silver arcs, like quiet rain.

### 3. Pull and lift

- **Says:** *Each footstep pulls the floor down a little, and a smooth force lifts it.* The first
  steps of the small primes press hardest. The lift comes from the smooth part of the formula and
  knows nothing about individual primes.
- **Shows:** each landing presses a soft dent into the floor, as deep as that footstep's pull on the
  box, 2Λ(n)/√n · (L − log n): the small primes' first steps press hardest, and a footstep near the
  far wall, where L − log n is small, barely dents it. A live line gives the two totals: *Far wall
  at 200: the footsteps pull 77.19 and the smooth force lifts 77.27.*

### 4. The springs

- **Says:** *Under the floor sit springs, one for each zeta zero. As the far wall moves out, each
  bounces at its own rate.* The far wall steps from 16 out to 64 and back, and every spring
  answers. How far a spring pushes depends only on where the far wall stands; its zero's height,
  14.13, 21.02, 25.01, …, sets how fast it bounces. There are infinitely many; the first 12 are
  shown.
- **Shows:** the lights fade, the floor turns translucent and the view turns side-on. Twelve
  springs stand beneath the floor, labelled with their zeros' heights. The far wall moves on the
  walkers' beat, one whole number per beat, from 16 out to 64 and back, with a short hold at each
  end. A thin floor line and a far-wall edge mark the hallway's length, and a number tag over the
  wall edge gives the whole number the wall stands at. Primes join and leave as the wall moves
  (six at 16, eighteen at 64) and each spring bounces at its own rate. The springs stand in one
  line and never slide; where a spring stands means nothing. The side view looks level and square
  onto that line, with no parallax, so a cap's height is its spring's push and nothing else. The
  frame is fixed at the longest hallway (64): only the wall, the walkers and the caps move.

### 5. The Riemann Hypothesis

- **Says:** *The Riemann Hypothesis says every spring stays steady, so the floor never cracks.*
  Riemann's explicit formula says the lift minus the footsteps equals the springs' total push,
  exactly, at every length. No spring sinks below its rest line, so the total never goes negative.
  This was proved via Lean 4 in November 2026.
- **Shows:** the same view, with the wall's clock running on from step 4 (it is not restarted, in
  either direction). A rest line appears across the spring row at the height of zero push: caps
  touch it and never pass below. The live line is the exact formula as a subtraction, rewritten
  once per beat when the wall's number changes: *Far wall at 64: lift 34.26 − footsteps 34.17 =
  0.09. The floor holds.* *Next* reads *What if a zero left the line?*

### 6. What if?

- **Says:** *If one zero left the line, a twin would come with it, and together they would pull
  the floor until it cracks.* The red pair sinks below the rest line. Only a what-if: the actual
  zeros all sit on the line, so the real floor holds.
- **Shows:** the first zero bent off the line by a = 0.4, its spring and its twin both red, side by
  side in the springs' line, their coils at least 10 px apart on screen. The wall stops and eases
  to 323, where the bent springs' total push is −0.0993: the red pair sinks below the rest line,
  the floor line turns red and the floor cracks. The live line reads *Far wall at 323: the
  springs' total push would be −0.10. The floor would crack.* It never shows a lift − footsteps
  subtraction, because the bent total is not G. Between 20 and 400 the bent total first turns
  negative at 84, but it swings back above zero between dips at 84–88, 128–137, 197–220 and
  300–344; 323 is the deepest point of the last dip. This is the only red moment in the piece.
  Going back heals the floor.

### 7. Each prime is simple

- **Says:** *Each prime on its own is simple: its trip has one of only two lengths, and its string
  rings with exactly known notes.* The miracle is how they all fit together.
- **Shows:** the lit hallway again, far wall at 200. The live line asks *Tap a string, or its
  number below, to pluck it.* A tapped string rings in its true vibration shapes, and the live
  line, in the same space so the card keeps its height, names the string and the shape. A row of
  buttons for 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 and 13 plucks from the keyboard, or on a phone, where the strings
  sit close together. Links to the proof note, to the computer check and to this project's Lean
  code. *Next* reads *Start over* and returns to step 1; the right arrow key does nothing here.

### How does this relate to the Riemann Hypothesis?

**Plain version.**
- Under the floor sits a row of hidden springs, one for each "zero" of the zeta function.
- The primes pull the floor down and a smooth background force lifts it. An exact formula,
  Riemann's explicit formula, says that the net result always equals the total push of the
  springs.
- A spring sitting exactly on the "critical line" can only push up. It bounces, but it never pulls
  below its rest line.
- A spring off the line would grow and start pulling the floor down until it cracks. That is
  step 6.
- So the Riemann Hypothesis is the statement that the floor never cracks: for every hallway length
  and every way you press, the primes never win.

**For the curious.**
- This is Weil's positivity criterion, in the finite "window" form Alain Connes proposed. In this
  repository it is the Lean theorem `Cvs.WeilForm.WindowWeilCriterion.rh_iff_windowPos` in
  `Cvs/WeilForm/WindowWeilCriterion.lean`, restated as `Cvs.rh_iff_weil_positivity` in
  `Cvs/Main.lean`. It proves that the Riemann Hypothesis holds exactly when Q(f) ≥ 0 on every
  window (0, log c] with c a whole number from c₀ on, for every f in that window's form domain.
  Any starting point c₀ ≥ 2 may be chosen, so checking only long hallways is enough.
- The form domain, `Cvs.WeilForm.FormDomain.formDomain` in `Cvs/WeilForm/FormDomain.lean`, holds
  every real square-integrable f on the window whose archimedean integral converges, so f need not
  be smooth or symmetric. Q keeps the primes up to a prime cut-off, here c, and the powers p^r with
  r up to a power cut-off, here ⌈log₂ c⌉. On any window (0, L],
  `Cvs.WeilForm.PrimeSideExplicitIndex.weilForm_eq_range` in
  `Cvs/WeilForm/PrimeSideExplicitIndex.lean` shows that the prime side is the sum over every prime
  power n < e^L, each with weight Λ(n)/√n, as long as the prime cut-off is at least e^L and the
  power cut-off at least L / log 2. Here L = log c and both hold, so Q's prime side runs over every
  prime power n < c.
- The criterion takes hallway lengths log c only for whole numbers c. At any real length L > 0, the
  Riemann Hypothesis gives Q ≥ 0 on the form domain of the window (0, L] whenever Q's prime cut-off
  is at least e^L and its power cut-off at least L / log 2:
  `Cvs.WeilForm.PositivityOfRH.Q_nonneg_of_RH` in `Cvs/WeilForm/PositivityOfRH.lean`. With the
  criterion, this makes the plain version's "every hallway length" equivalent to the Riemann
  Hypothesis as well.
- Levent Alpöge's proof with Claude, proved via Lean 4 in November 2026, shows the floor holds.

### What does the path law do?

**Plain version.** The path law is why each walker behaves so neatly:
- in any hallway, a prime's trip has only two possible lengths;
- its string therefore has exactly known vibration shapes;
- its pull has a hard ceiling it can approach but never exceed.

The path law is the physics of a single walker.

**What it does not do, which is the interesting part.**
- Knowing every walker's ceiling is not enough to save the floor. Add up all the ceilings and the
  total is far more than the floor can hold, and the gap keeps growing as the hallway gets longer.
- The floor survives because the walkers' pulls partly cancel, like voices in a choir that never
  all hit their loudest note at once.
- The Riemann Hypothesis lives in that cancellation between primes, not in any one prime. The path
  law explains the soloists; the Riemann Hypothesis is about the choir.

**For the curious.**
- Take a whole number c ≥ 2. Each prime power q < c adds one term to the prime side of the Weil
  form on the window (0, log c]. Rescale the shape to f on [0, 1) and that term is ⟨A_q f, f⟩ for
  one operator, the atom A_q = (Λ(q)/√q)·W_β with β = log q / log c; W_β adds the shifts of f by
  +β and −β, each cut off where it would leave [0, 1)
  (`Cvs.WeilForm.MaskedShift.PrimeAtomRealisation.atom` in
  `Cvs/WeilForm/MaskedShift/PrimeAtomRealisation.lean`, `Cvs.PrimeOperator.term_eq` in
  `Cvs/WeilForm/MaskedShift/PrimeSideAtomForm.lean`). The rescaling keeps norms, and the whole
  prime side is the sum of the atoms' forms
  (`Cvs.WeilForm.WindowCircleIsometry.norm_sq_windowCircle` and
  `Cvs.WeilForm.WindowCircleIsometry.primeTerm_rep_eq_inner_primeOperator` in
  `Cvs/WeilForm/WindowCircleIsometry.lean`).
- Steps of β cut [0, 1) into chains r, r + β, r + 2β, … (0 ≤ r < β) that stay inside it, and
  every chain has m or m − 1 points, m = ⌈log c / log q⌉ (`Cvs.PathLaw.chainLength_eq_or` and
  `Cvs.PathLaw.capacity_one_div` in `Cvs/PathLaw/ThreeDistance.lean`). A path of m points has
  vibration shapes sin(πjk/(m+1)) and notes 2cos(πj/(m+1)), j, k = 1, …, m
  (`Cvs.PathLaw.hasEigenvector_pathAdj` in `Cvs/PathLaw/Spectrum.lean`). On each chain W_β acts
  as the path graph on its points, and when both lengths occur its spectrum is exactly the notes
  of the two paths (`Cvs.PathLaw.wStep_iterate`, `Cvs.PathLaw.coeFn_pathLawW_pow` and
  `Cvs.PathLaw.spectrum_pathLawW` in `Cvs/PathLaw/SpectralSet.lean`). Lean proves these last two
  facts for `Cvs.PathLaw.pathLawW` in `Cvs/PathLaw/Weight.lean`, a second definition of W_β, and
  does not state that it equals the atom's.
- The atom's strength, its operator norm, is exactly 2cos(π/(m+1))·Λ(q)/√q for every 1 < q < c.
  This is proved for the atom itself (`Cvs.Operator.norm_atom` in
  `Cvs/WeilForm/MaskedShift/AtomNormLaw.lean`). When √c ≤ q < c, m = 2 and 2cos(π/3) = 1
  (`Cvs.Operator.chebFactor_two` in `Cvs/WeilForm/MaskedShift/ChebFactorMonotone.lean`), so the
  strength is exactly Λ(q)/√q.
- Adding up the strengths bounds the primes' pull on the flat box of length log c:
  2·Σ_{n≤c} Λ(n)/√n · log(c/n) is at most log c times the sum of the strengths
  (`Cvs.Route.SpectralChainThreeCut.two_mul_Psihalf_le_log_mul_atomBudget` in
  `Cvs/Route/SpectralChainThreeCut.lean`). The bound overshoots the true pull, by a factor of 1.7
  at c = 10, 3.5 at c = 10⁶ and 4.1 at c = 10⁷, close to log c / 4 for large c.
  The smooth lift beats the pull by only 0.05 to 0.14 (the box energy G(log c), at every whole
  number c from 2 to 10⁶), so for every c from 3 to 10⁶ the strengths cannot even show
  G(log c) ≥ 0. These numbers are computed, not proved. The Riemann Hypothesis is exactly the
  statement that the box energy is never negative, G(L) ≥ 0 for every L > 0
  (`Cvs.Route.OneCellCriterion.riemannHypothesis_iff_G_nonneg` in
  `Cvs/Route/OneCellCriterion.lean`), so strength bounds alone cannot prove it.
- Alpöge's proof handles the cancellation with deep arithmetic: a "tower" of symmetries that makes
  all the primes cooperate. His warm-up model even uses the same path-shaped pattern,
  x₁x₂ + x₂x₃ + …, as the walkers' strings.

## UX principles

- **One idea per step.** The story shows one new thing at a time, and the story is the whole page.
- **Instant feedback.** Every action answers within 100 ms with motion, sound or a number.
- **No equations by default.** Numbers appear only in the story's live line and the far-wall tag.
  A *Show the math* drawer that overlays the exact formulas on the live scene is planned.
- **The live line.** Steps 3, 5 and 6 carry one line of numbers under the card's text. It always
  begins *Far wall at N:*, gives figures to two decimals with a true minus sign (U+2212), and is
  worked out with the wall resting on N, so every line shown is a true statement. It is rewritten
  only when N changes. While the hallway eases to a new length it speaks of the length it is
  easing to, so it never races through the numbers in between. It keeps one height through a step
  (that of its text at 64 where the wall moves), so the card and the frame above it hold still.
- **The far-wall tag.** In the side view (steps 4, 5 and 6) a small number stands over the top of
  the wall edge: the same whole number the live line speaks of (323 in step 6). It is a page
  element, not part of the 3D scene, so it stays sharp; it stays inside the viewport and clear of
  the card, fades in as the lights go down, and steps out while the hallway eases to a new length.
- **The rest line.** One thin line across the whole spring row, the twin included, at the height
  of zero push, at least 2 px thick. It shows only on steps 5 and 6. "The line" in card copy
  always means the critical line; this one is always called the "rest line".
- **Lights.** In the side X-ray view the hallway's lamps and their glow fade out, so nothing
  streaks the floor or walls; the springs, arcs, rest line, floor line and wall edge are unlit
  and stay as they are. The floor line and the wall edge, at least 1.5 px thick, keep the
  hallway's length readable with the lights down.
- **Sound starts on the first tap.** Every sound has a caption. (Planned; the page is silent so far.)
- **Phone:** the story card sits at the bottom and the hallway frames itself in the space above it.
  On an upright screen the story trades its wide views for ones that fill a tall frame: the start
  of the floor in step 1, the view down the hallway in steps 2, 3 and 7, and in the side view
  springs that hang up to three and a half times longer, the rest line lowered with them so that a
  full push still ends below the floor. The side view fits everything from the tops of the arcs
  down to the zero heights between the title and the card, with room for the wall tag; where the
  band below the title is short, as on a phone held sideways, the hallway stands beside the title
  or the whole title block instead. Steps 4 and 5 share one frame, sized for the taller of their
  cards, so the picture holds still from one to the other. On an upright phone the wall moves only
  a few pixels per beat, so the ticking tag, the hops and the springs are the per-beat cue.
  Nothing depends on hover.
- **Labels:** numerals, footprint numbers, zero heights and walker tags keep one size on screen at
  any distance, outlined so they read over ticks and threads. Where two would overlap, the less
  important one steps aside: zero heights first, then footprint numbers (every walker's first
  steps before anyone's later ones, each walker's shown as an unbroken run from its first step),
  then the numerals; no label sits under the title or the card. In step 2, where only 2 and 3 are
  numbered, 3's numbers stand above its footprints, so the two runs never meet. While the springs
  show, the numerals, footprint numbers and walker tags step back. Walker tags give way only to
  each other, the smaller prime first, and a walker fading out at its last footprint keeps its
  tag's place for its next trip. Which labels show is decided for where the camera is heading
  (between steps, the length the hallway is easing to), a label keeps its place until it overlaps
  by 3 px, one hidden earlier in the step returns only once it is 3 px clear, and labels fade in
  and out, so none flickers. The zero heights take as many staggered rows as clear the most of
  them where the springs stand in that step, in a smaller face on a short screen; where they
  cannot all clear, as on a phone held sideways, a fixed set shows for the whole step, the first
  zeros first.
- **First paint:** the page opens with step 1 already on the card, before the 3D library has
  loaded. *Next* waits, disabled, until the hallway is drawn; if it cannot be drawn, a message
  says why and the step buttons go away.
- **Desktop:** a slight parallax follows the pointer, except in the X-ray side view, which stays
  square.
- **Accessibility:**
  - colour-blind-safe hues for the small primes, on every pair of lanes since their threads cross;
    a calm warm grey for the big ones;
  - a reduced-motion mode (`prefers-reduced-motion`) that swaps animation for stepped states: the
    walkers stand still; in steps 4 and 5 the far wall shows only the stops 16, 20, 25, 32, 40,
    50 and 64, out and back, 1.6 s each (in the what-if step it stands at 323); the springs, the
    lights, the rest line, the wall tag, the crack and the hallway's length all switch without
    easing; the tag and the live line still update at each stop; step 3 holds its deepest presses
    at once, and a plucked string holds its shape;
  - every interaction reachable by keyboard: arrow keys for the story (with Alt, Ctrl, Shift or
    the Command key they are left to the browser), and *Pluck a string* buttons in step 7's card;
    focus never falls back to the page when the control it sits on goes away (a button disabled,
    the pluck row hidden, a link rewritten): it moves to *Next*.
- **Performance:** 60 fps on a mid-range phone. While the wall moves, the scene's structure
  changes only on downbeats.

## Art direction

**The rule:** all the beauty comes from the real structure and none from decoration. The
mathematics already makes striking patterns; the visuals light them well.

### Principles

1. **Emergence over ornament.** One walker is simple. Forty walkers obeying the two-length rule
   weave a rhythmic tapestry across the hallway, like threads on a loom. The pattern is real (it is
   the three-distance structure) and needs nothing added.
2. **One idea per frame.** Light the one thing being explained; let everything else sink into soft,
   dim context. Richness lives in the background, comprehension in the foreground.
3. **One meaning per visual channel, the same in every step:**
   - *hue:* which prime (jewel tones for small primes, silver-grey for large ones);
   - *brightness:* activity;
   - *thread thickness:* pull strength, Λ(q)/√q;
   - *floor height:* the energy, and a footstep's dent its pull;
   - *pitch:* the string's real eigenvalues;
   - *red:* only the crack.
4. **Transitions are explanations.** Morph instead of cutting: footprints bead into a string, the
   string vibrates, the lights fade and the floor turns translucent to show the springs, and between
   steps the hallway eases to its new length inside a frame already set for it; the numerals and
   the wall tag wait until it arrives.
   One object becomes the next idea.
5. **Point to learn.** Pause and tap anything glowing for one sentence about what it is, with its
   formula highlighted in the math drawer. If a visual cannot be explained in one tap-sentence, cut
   it.

### Signature visual per step

1. **A slide-rule floor:** the landings of 2 light up at 2, 4, 8, 16, equal steps apart on a floor
   numbered like a slide rule.
2. **Every prime walks:** walkers fade in lane by lane; with many walkers the two-length pattern
   becomes the loom tapestry, the hero image of the piece.
3. **Pull and lift:** each landing presses a soft dent into the membrane, as deep as its pull,
   deepest for the small primes' first steps, and fades.
4. **The springs:** an x-ray transition fades the lights, turns the floor translucent and reveals
   twelve springs, each labelled with its zero; the far wall ticks out and back on the walkers'
   beat and the springs bounce at visibly different rates.
5. **The Riemann Hypothesis:** the rest line appears under the caps; none ever passes below it,
   while the live subtraction changes with every beat and its total stays positive.
6. **What if?:** the bent spring and its twin turn red and sink below the rest line; the floor
   line turns red, tension lines gather on the membrane, glow red and fracture. Going back heals
   the floor.
7. **Each prime is simple:** a tapped string rings in its true mode shapes, at rates taken from its
   real spectrum. Planned: above it, a slowly rotating regular polygon casts shadows that land
   exactly on the string's notes, because a path's eigenvalues are projections of a polygon's
   corners.

### Palette and materials

- **Ground:** deep charcoal-indigo walls, a warm amber floor light.
- **Primes:** jewel tones for the small primes that stay apart for colour-blind viewers on every
  pair, since they differ in lightness as well as hue; a warm silver for the large ones, which fall
  like quiet rain.
- **Floor:** a smooth membrane with a subtle grid, physically lit.
- **Alert:** red, reserved for the crack.

### Toolkit (Three.js)

- **Instancing:** instanced meshes for thousands of footprints and beads; tube geometry for the
  threads.
- **Lighting:** a warm key light on the floor, cool fill on the walls, ACES tone mapping.
- **Depth and focus:** gentle depth of field; light haze held to the camera's distance, so a look
  reads the same at every window size.
- **Glow with restraint:** bloom on active elements only (thread heads, landings, walkers, the
  crack), rendered separately so lights and highlights never glow. Glow carries meaning; it is not
  a filter over everything.
- **Membrane:** a vertex-displaced plane that dents under the pointer and under each footstep.
- **Camera:** mostly side-on (2.5D) so the structure stays legible, slight parallax for depth, slow
  dolly moves for scale.

### Sound

Planned:
- Each string's notes come from its real spectrum, so longer strings sound like richer chords.
- Notes trigger light pulses on their string, so sound and sight confirm each other.
- Footsteps are soft wooden ticks; the crack is a low, short thud.

### Avoid

- Particles or motion that map to nothing.
- Glow everywhere.
- Many colours meaning many things.
- Text over moving content.
- Camera moves the viewer did not ask for during an explanation.
- A second meter, a trace graph, or any readout beside the live line and the wall tag.
- Springs standing beyond the far wall, or tied to a footprint.

## Computing it for real

- **Walkers and strings:** exact, from m = ⌈log c / log q⌉ and the path-graph eigenvectors. The
  walker p lands at log p^k for k = 0, 1, … while p^k < c.
- **Floor height:** the Weil form `Cvs.WeilForm.SelfConvolutionFunctional.weilForm P R f`
  (`Cvs/WeilForm/SelfConvolutionFunctional.lean`), whose whole-number cutoffs P and R limit the
  primes and their powers:

  Q(f) = 2(∫ f e^{u/2})(∫ f e^{−u/2}) + A₀ g(0) + ∫₀^∞ e^{−v/4}/(1 − e^{−v}) (g(0) − g(v/2)) dv
  − Σ_{p≤P} Σ_{r=1}^{R} (log p) p^{−r/2} (g(r log p) + g(−r log p)),

  where g(t) = ∫ f(u) f(u + t) du is the autocorrelation of f, p runs over the primes and
  A₀ = ψ(¼) − log π. With n = p^r the weight (log p) p^{−r/2} is Λ(n)/√n. For L ≥ 0, e^L ≤ P,
  L ≤ R log 2 and f = 0 off (0, L], `Cvs.WeilForm.PrimeSideExplicitIndex.weilForm_eq_range`
  (`Cvs/WeilForm/PrimeSideExplicitIndex.lean`) rewrites the prime sum as
  Σ_{n<⌈e^L⌉} Λ(n)/√n (g(log n) + g(−log n)). That is the full sum over all n, since g(t) = 0
  once |t| ≥ L. `Cvs.WeilForm.WindowWeilCriterion.rh_iff_windowPos`
  (`Cvs/WeilForm/WindowWeilCriterion.lean`) proves, for any whole x₀ ≥ 2, that RH holds exactly
  when Q(f) ≥ 0 at L = log x, P = x and R = ⌈log₂ x⌉ for every whole x ≥ x₀ and every
  square-integrable f that vanishes off (0, L] and has a finite v-integral.
- **Box energy (the live line's total):** for the box of length L = log c, computed exactly from the primes:

  G(L) = 8√c + A₀L + 2C₀ − 2 Σ_{k≥1} c^{−(2k+½)}/(2k+½)² − 2 Σ_{n≤c} Λ(n)/√n · (L − log n),

  with A₀ = ψ(¼) − log π and C₀ = ψ′(¼)/4 − 8. This is `Cvs.Route.OneCellLaplaceTransform.G`
  (`Cvs/Route/OneCellLaplaceTransform.lean`), defined there as
  16(cosh(L/2) − 1) + A₀L + 2 Σ_{k≥0} (1 − c^{−(2k+½)})/(2k+½)² − 2 Σ_n Λ(n)/√n (L − log n)₊;
  expanding the cosh and using Σ_{k≥0} (2k+½)^{−2} = ψ′(¼)/4 gives the line above.
  `Cvs.Route.OneCellBoxWindowForm.weilForm_flat_eq` (`Cvs/Route/OneCellBoxWindowForm.lean`)
  proves that G(L) = Q(f) for the box f = 1 on (0, L], 0 elsewhere, whenever L > 0, e^L ≤ P and
  L ≤ R log 2. `Cvs.Route.OneCellCriterion.riemannHypothesis_iff_G_nonneg`
  (`Cvs/Route/OneCellCriterion.lean`) proves that RH holds exactly when G(L) ≥ 0 for every L > 0.
  On the zero side, `Cvs.Route.OneCellExplicitFormula.zeroSideG`
  (`Cvs/Route/OneCellExplicitFormula.lean`) proves, with no assumption, that
  G(L) = Σ_ρ m_ρ Re[4 sin²(zL/2)/z²] for every L ≥ 0, where ρ runs over the nontrivial zeros of
  ζ, m_ρ is its multiplicity and z = −i(ρ − ½). A zero ½ + iγ on the line has z = γ; with its
  conjugate ½ − iγ it adds 8 sin²(γL/2)/γ². With every zero on the line,
  G(L) = Σ_{γ>0} 8 sin²(γL/2)/γ², each γ counted with its multiplicity.
- **The balance (steps 3 and 5):** G splits into the footsteps' pull and the smooth lift,
  G(L) = lift − footsteps, with

  footsteps = 2 Σ_{n≤c} Λ(n)/√n · (L − log n),
  lift = 8√c + A₀L + 2C₀ − 2 Σ_{k≥1} c^{−(2k+½)}/(2k+½)²,

  the lift being every term of G that does not depend on the primes. At 200 the lift is 77.2722,
  the footsteps 77.1935 and G = 0.0787170. The page rounds the lift and G to two places and shows
  the footsteps as their difference, so the subtraction on screen is exact: at 16 it reads
  9.70 − 9.61 = 0.09, at 17 10.36 − 10.23 = 0.13, at 64 34.26 − 34.17 = 0.09. The shown footsteps
  figure can therefore differ from the footsteps' own rounding by 0.01 (at 9 of the 49 whole
  numbers from 16 to 64) and is never more than 0.0069 from the true value. Each footstep's dent
  in step 3 is in proportion to its own term of the footsteps' sum. The floor holds 32 dents at a
  time; when a new one lands, the dent with the least depth left gives way, so none vanishes while
  it can still be seen.
- **The far wall's clock (steps 4 and 5):** the wall runs on the walkers' beat (0.62 s). Counted
  in beats, it rests on a whole number at every downbeat and moves to the next one during the
  beat, eased in log c with the walkers' hop ease: 16 out to 64 in 48 beats, a hold of 2 beats,
  back in 48, a hold of 2 (100 beats, 62 s). The hallway's length is continuous in between, and
  everything drawn uses that length. The wall rests a hair past each whole number n, so the
  footprint at n is inside the hallway and in the sum; the number shown, on the tag and in the
  live line, is the largest whole number inside the hallway. Entering from another step, the
  hallway eases to 16 and holds there three to four beats while the camera settles; moving
  between steps 4 and 5 leaves the clock alone. Walkers keep their place in their hop when their
  chain gains or loses a footprint, and a prime the wall has just let in joins on a downbeat, so
  every landing stays on the beat.
- **The springs (steps 4 to 6):** the first 12 zeros are shown, γ = 14.13 to 56.45. Each spring's
  height is sin²(γ ln(c)/2), its push 8 sin²(γL/2)/γ² rescaled by γ²/8 so that every spring swings
  between 0 and 1; brightness falls off like 1/γ. While the wall's clock runs, each on-line spring
  is driven directly by the wall's position, with no follower and so no lag: it moves during the
  beat and pauses on the downbeat, as the wall does. One way from 16 to 64 the first spring makes
  3.12 bounces and the twelfth 12.45. Everywhere else (a step change, the hold before the clock
  starts, the bent pair) a spring follows its target critically damped and stops at the rest line
  if it comes down to it, so an on-line spring never dips below the line; while the hallway eases
  to a new length the springs glide once to their pushes there. The springs are spread along the
  floor of the shortest hallway of the step (16 on steps 4 and 5) and do not move when the wall
  does. Where a tall screen stretches the springs by a factor k, the rest line drops by the extra
  bounce, 0.8(k − 1), so a full push still tops out 0.25 below the floor. Only the bent pair can
  push harder than a full push; past it a spring rises a third as fast and is not stretched, so
  even the bent pair's cap stays below the floor.
- **The crack (step 6):** computed on the zero side. An off-line zero at ½ + a + iγ arrives with
  its twin at ½ − a + iγ, and the pair replaces that zero's on-line spring 8 sin²(γL/2)/γ² with
  16 Re[sin²(zL/2)/z²], z = γ − ia. With the first zero (γ ≈ 14.13) bent off the line, the box
  energy G first dips below zero at L ≈ 15.5 when a = 0.1, 8.0 when a = 0.2, 5.3 when a = 0.3 and
  4.4 when a = 0.4. The pair's own term goes negative much earlier, near L = 2π/γ. With a = 0.4 and
  whole c from 20 to 400, G is negative only for c in 84–88, 128–137, 197–220 and 300–344, and
  lowest at c = 323 (G = −0.0993), where step 6 stands. The crack shows exactly when the total on
  show is negative: it opens slowly, closes fast, and the floor line reddens and thickens with it,
  so it reads even on a phone, where the side view sees the floor edge-on. The bend is a switch,
  not an animation; only the twin's fade and the red are eased.

## Accuracy guardrails

- Do not say the path law proves the Riemann Hypothesis. It describes each prime exactly; the
  proof needs the cancellation between primes.
- The approved sentence about the proof is the one on the step-5 card: "This was proved via Lean 4
  in November 2026." Quote it as it stands, with links; do not write "solved forever" or
  "confirmed by experts", and do not reword it.
- The crack is a hypothetical. Label it as what would happen if the hypothesis were false, and
  never show a lift − footsteps subtraction for the bent total.
- Footprints land on prime powers, not multiples. Never draw a walker stepping on 6 or 10.
- A spring's position carries no meaning; its zero's height is its bounce rate. The springs stand
  under the shortest hallway and stay there. Never place a spring at a footprint or suggest the
  zeros sit along the hallway.
- Springs answer the far wall's length, never a single footstep.
- The shared beat is a pacing choice: the wall is moved on the walkers' beat. The mathematics has
  no beat, and the copy must not suggest it has.
- A footprint joins the sum on the downbeat the far wall reaches it; its walker reaches it within
  the next one to three beats, always on a downbeat. Do not claim the two are simultaneous.
- Every number shown is computed by the page from the formulas above, with the wall resting on
  the whole number named. The shown footsteps figure is the shown lift minus the shown total, so
  it can differ from its own rounding by 0.01.
- The floor line turning red is the crack, shown exactly when the total push is negative.
- The letter c never appears on screen; the wording is "Far wall at N".
